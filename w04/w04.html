Where we left off

Week 3 turned single estimates into confidence intervals and compared AfD support between East and West German counties — one variable, split into two groups. Slope and intercept

The OLS slope is the sample covariance of and , scaled by the sample variance of :

counties |>
  summarize(beta_1 = cov(fdp, greens) / var(fdp))
# A tibble: 1 × 1
  beta_1
   <dbl>
1   2.75

Both and divide by , which cancels. Written out, the slope is a ratio of two sums of deviations from the means:

We build it step by step, starting with the two means:

mean_x <- counties |>
  summarize(mean_x = mean(fdp)) |>
  pull(mean_x)
mean_x
[1] 4.13741

mean_y <- counties |>
  summarize(mean_y = mean(greens)) |>
  pull(mean_y)
mean_y
[1] 10.05147

Per county, the deviations from the means, their product, and the squared deviation:

ols_hand <- counties |>
  select(county_code, fdp, greens) |>
  mutate(
    diff_x         = fdp - mean_x,

The intercept then makes the line pass through the point of means , reusing both means and the slope:

beta_0 <- mean_y - beta_1 * mean_x

[1] -1.335843

On average, a county with one percentage point more FDP support has 2.75 points more Greens support. The intercept, -1.34, is the predicted Greens share at 0% FDP — an impossible negative share, and far outside the data (no county has the FDP below 2%). Intercepts are often not meaningful on their own.

Goodness of fit

How much of the variation in Greens support does the line explain? Every county’s distance from the mean splits into a part the line explains and a residual part. Per county, we add the explained deviation and the squares of all three parts to ols_hand — the total deviation (diff_y) and the residual are already there:

ols_hand <- ols_hand |>

    diff_hat_squared = diff_hat^2,
    residual_squared = residual^2

For eight counties spread across the range of FDP shares, the three parts as segments, side by side: the total deviation from the mean to the county (centre), the residual from the line to the county (left), and the explained part from the mean up to the line (right):

Summing each squared column over all counties gives three sums of squares. The total sum of squares is the variation in itself, the explained sum of squares the variation the line reproduces, and the residual sum of squares what’s left over — the quantity OLS minimized:

ss_sums <- ols_hand |>
  summarize(
    tss = sum(diff_y_squared),
    ess = sum(diff_hat_squared),
    rss = sum(residual_squared)
  )

r_squared <- ess / tss

Equivalently, one minus the unexplained share:

The correlation coefficient measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship on a scale from to . With a single predictor, is just the squared correlation, so is its square root — positive here, since it takes the sign of the slope:

Regression tables

Papers report models as regression tables. modelsummary() builds one from a fitted model: coef_map renames (and selects) the coefficients, gof_map picks the fit statistics, and statistic = NULL and stars = FALSE leave out the standard errors and significance stars for now (Week 6):

modelsummary(
  fdp_model,
  output    = "tinytable",
  coef_map  = c(
    "(Intercept)" = "Intercept",
    "fdp"         = "FDP vote share (%)"
  ),
  statistic = NULL,
  stars     = FALSE,
  gof_map   = c("nobs", "r.squared"),
  fmt       = 3
)
(1)
Intercept -1.336
FDP vote share (%) 2.752
Num.Obs. 400
R2 0.314

With output = "tinytable", the table adapts to whatever format the document is rendered to — an HTML table here, a Typst table in the PDF, same code. See the modelsummary documentation for everything else it can do.

Residual plot

Plotting residuals against shows what the line systematically misses. Ideally they scatter evenly around zero, like pure noise:

A dashed OLS line fitted through the residuals themselves (geom_smooth()) lies exactly on the zero line: OLS residuals are uncorrelated with by construction.

fdp_fit |>
  ggplot(aes(x = fdp, y = .resid)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.4) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, color = course_primary, linewidth = 0.8) +
  geom_smooth(
    method   = "lm",
    formula  = y ~ x,
    se       = FALSE,
    color    = "firebrick",
    linetype = "dashed"
  ) +
  labs(
    title = "Residuals of the Greens-FDP model",

  ) +
  theme_intror()

Not quite noise: residuals are mostly negative at both ends of the FDP range and positive in the middle — the relationship bends, which a straight line can’t follow.

Interpreting a log coefficient

With a logged predictor, no longer describes a one-unit step in density, but a proportional one. Comparing two counties whose densities differ by a factor :

The starting density drops out: going from 100 to 200 residents per km² changes the prediction exactly as much as going from 1,000 to 2,000.

Doubling a county’s density (), with log_beta_1 from the log model above:

log_beta_1 * log(2)
[1] 1.890443

A county twice as dense as another is predicted to vote 1.89 percentage points more Green — wherever on the density scale the two counties sit.

A tenfold density () is one gridline step in the log-axis plot above:

log_beta_1 * log(10)
[1] 6.279917

A 10% denser county (), roughly :

log_beta_1 * log(1.1)
[1] 0.2599426

This gives a rule of thumb for a logged predictor: a 10% increase in goes along with a change of about units in — here, about 0.273 percentage points of Greens support, close to the 0.260 computed above (the gap comes from rather than 0.1).

The same density increase in absolute terms — say, 100 more residents per km² — means a lot in a rural county and almost nothing in a city. That diminishing effect is exactly what the log model captures.

Concluding remarks

You’ve fitted a regression line by hand, decomposed its fit into TSS, ESS and RSS, reproduced everything with lm() and broom, and used a log transformation to fit a curved relationship with a linear model. Every model today used a single predictor for all 400 counties at once; whether a relationship holds up once we also account for other differences between counties — East vs. West, for instance — is the question of statistical control in Week 5.
